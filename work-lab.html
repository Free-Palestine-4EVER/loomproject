Sixteen cards. All of them, all at once.

Three card walls, same sixteen jobs, same photography, same words. Every one of them puts all sixteen cases on screen together — nothing behind a click, nothing off the right edge, no “load more”. What changes is the card itself: what it leads with, how big it is allowed to be, how much it says without being asked, and whether the sixteen are one flat set or sorted into groups.

They are deliberately three different answers, not three skins: a name wall, a picture wall, and a map. Each is honestly better at some things and worse at others — the note under each one says which.

01

The Label Wall

Sixteen identical squares, laid out like a sheet of woven care-labels — the studio's own felt-and-yarn material, one selvedge stripe of coloured yarn per card. The card leads with the client's name set large, then the line, the disciplines and the market: at rest this is a wall of names, and the range of the roster is the first thing the eye reads, not any one photograph. The photography is still there — it fills the card the moment you stop on it, with the felt and the type sitting on top. Uniform size is a statement in itself: no case is ranked above another, a Benetton square is exactly the size of a Sarajevo bakery square. The trade: it is the coldest of the three on arrival — a studio that sells visual craft is showing type first, and a visitor who never moves the pointer sees very little art. Long client names ("Maison de l'Avenir", "ModulArt · Liberty Group") fight the big type, and at 390px the squares are small enough that the scope chips have to be dropped.

02

The Mosaic

One picture wall in four card sizes, and the size is the argument — except the argument never sits still. The wall is split into three blocks of two rows each, and a block re-tiles every 2.6 seconds: a standard card opens into a wide band, a band stands up into a tower, the flagship draws itself back down to make room. Each move takes 3.4 seconds, which is longer than the gap between them — so the next block sets off while the last is still settling and there is never a frame with nothing in flight. Grid spans can't be animated, so each move is done by FLIP — measure every box, swap the classes, measure again, then run the moved cards from their old box to their new one on transform alone, with the photograph counter-scaled so it re-crops instead of stretching. And under all of that the photographs are alive: every card runs a permanent Ken-Burns drift of its own — a pan under a percent and a few points of zoom, over 27 to 48 seconds, each card on its own period, its own bearing and its own phase, so the sixteen never once fall into step. The wall answers the pointer too — each crop leans toward the cursor by an amount that falls off with distance, and the fifteen cards you are not on step back, which turns sixteen framed pictures into one surface you are moving over. All sixteen cards are on screen in every state and in every frame of every move: each block's maps were built to fill their two rows exactly, so the wall is always six rows tall and nothing under it ever shifts. Attention overrides the cycle — hover or tab to a card and its block walks to the map that gives that card the most room, and the re-tiling stands down while your pointer is in the wall, though the breathing never does. The trade: it publicly ranks your own clients, and now it does so out loud — a card that shrinks while you're reading it is a card telling you it matters less. It also depends entirely on the covers being strong, and it costs the most of the three: it is the only one of the three walls that is never at rest. Below 1001px the re-tiling settles — at one or two columns a "tall" card is just a column-wide card — but the breathing stays, because on a phone it is the whole design and only the two cards actually on screen are ever running.

The Atlas

The same sixteen cards, clustered by the market each one shipped into — Bosnia, the Gulf, Jordan, Central Europe, North America — with a count on every cluster heading. The card turns on its side: a square of the work on the left, and on the right the client, the line, every discipline and the year, all of it on the face. Nothing is hidden behind a hover, which makes this the only one of the three that reads identically on a phone, in a screenshot, and to a screen reader. It is also the one that answers the question a prospect actually asks — have you worked in my market? — and the clusters make "nine countries" something you can count rather than something we claim. The trade: it is the least dramatic wall of the three — clean editorial cards, small images, no single hero moment, and it is the tallest section on the page because every card carries its full text. It also hard-codes a grouping that flatters the biggest cluster (six Bosnian cases) and leaves the USA on its own with a single card.
