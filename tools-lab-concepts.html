Six tools we built for ourselves.

Three ways to present the 3D Lab. The section's job is not to sell six products — none of these are for sale. It is to prove that the studio can build the thing it is asking you to buy. Each concept below makes that argument differently, and each is a working prototype with the real screenshots and the real copy. SPLAT LAB has no screenshot, so watch how each concept handles the gap — that is a design decision, not an omission.

01

The suite

The six tools stop being six cards and become one piece of software with six modules — a window, a sidebar, tabs, a viewport and a console that types out what the selected tool does. It makes the strongest version of the argument: this is not a shelf of experiments, it is an internal toolchain, and it is running right now. The console line does the work a paragraph of marketing can't — it reads as output, not as claim. What it's bad at: only one tool is visible at a time, so a scanner never sees all six at once; the chrome is a costume, and a client who reads it as a literal product screenshot will ask where they can download it; and it is the least brand-like of the three — it borrows a developer's visual language, not LOOM's.

02

The bench

A spatial layout instead of a grid: the tools sit as instruments on one continuous work surface, plugged into a shared power rail, each with a bezelled screen, a vent-slotted top plate, a status lamp and feet. Powering one on lights its lamp, brings its screen up to full and slides its description over the display. It says workshop the way the IDE says engineering, it is the most physical and the most ownable of the three, and it survives a tool with no screenshot better than anything else here — that instrument simply has a dot-matrix readout instead of a display, which is what real rigs look like. What it's bad at: it costs a lot of pixels per tool, so it needs horizontal scroll and a portion of the six is always off-screen; the skeuomorphism is a commitment and will date; and the screenshots end up small, which is a real loss when the screenshots are the proof.

03

The dossier

Every tool gets a full-width record instead of a card: an outlined index number, a spec table built from the real fields, the name at display size, and the screenshot filed as a plate with crop marks and a caption slug. The tag taxonomy becomes a working filter across the top, so the section can be read as "what does this studio actually cover" — generative 3D, WebGL, spatial AI, capture, architecture, design systems — which is precisely the capability argument. It is the calmest, the most legible on a phone, and the only one where each screenshot is big enough to be evidence. What it's bad at: it is long — six full-width records is a lot of scroll inside a page that already has sixteen sections; it reads as a catalogue, which is the exact framing we said we didn't want; and the filter is a promise of depth the section may not be able to keep with only six entries.
